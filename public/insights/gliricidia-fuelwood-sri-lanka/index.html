Gliricidia and fuelwood for industrial biomass in Sri Lanka

Why Gliricidia and other fuelwoods suit biomass gasifiers in Sri Lanka: coppicing, moisture, sizing, energy content and how to secure a reliable supply.

EnerFab engineering
14 November 2026
3 min read

Key points

  • Gliricidia is a fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing tree that regrows after cutting, so it can be harvested again and again.
  • Moisture is the biggest factor in fuel quality: downdraft gasifiers need wood below 25% moisture (dry basis), so fresh wood must be dried.
  • Dry wood of most species holds a broadly similar amount of energy per kilogram, roughly 18 to 20 MJ.
  • A biomass plant needs real control over its fuel supply; EnerFab recommends at least half.

Why Gliricidia

Gliricidia sepium is a leguminous tree, native to Mexico and Central America, that is now common across Sri Lanka as a live fence and a shade tree in plantations. Three things make it a good energy crop:

  • It coppices. When the stems are cut, the stump sends up new shoots, so the same trees can be harvested repeatedly without replanting. This is called short-rotation coppicing.
  • It fixes nitrogen. Like other legumes, it adds nitrogen to the soil, and its leaves are widely used as green manure.
  • It grows fast on land that is too poor for many other crops.

What makes good fuelwood

FactorWhy it matters
MoistureWater in the wood has to be evaporated before it can release energy. Freshly cut wood often contains as much water as dry matter. The FAO guide says downdraft gasifiers need fuel below 25% moisture (dry basis), so wood is air-dried first.
SizeGasifiers need evenly sized pieces so the fuel bed flows and air passes through evenly. Sticks are chopped into small blocks.
CleanlinessSoil and stones add ash and can form slag. Fuel is kept off the ground and handled carefully.
Energy contentCompletely dry wood of most species contains roughly 18 to 20 MJ per kilogram. The usable energy falls as moisture rises.

Other biomass fuels

EnerFab’s supply base handles Gliricidia, Ipil Ipil, cinnamon wood, timber off-cuts, coconut shells and other approved fuelwood. Each has its own advantages, growing areas and season, and a good supplier plans around all three.

A model energy plantation

EnerFab established a model fuelwood plantation at Nawagaththegama in the Puttalam district in the Maha season of 2008: 40,000 plants at 2 by 1 metre spacing, on land that had been scrubland. The plantation is now mature. It enriches the soil with nitrogen, supplies fuel to industry and improves the local microclimate.

Securing a reliable supply

A biomass plant is only as reliable as its fuel. If you invest in a power plant, you need real control over your fuelwood supply. Authorities expect it when you seek approvals, and if outside suppliers fall short, your own supply has to carry the plant. In EnerFab’s view, a plant should control at least 50% of its own supply.

EnerFab collects biomass through a network of 3,000 farmers and a supplier base of more than 2,000 across Sri Lanka, outside the North and East, with its own transport that can scale up or down. Its DM500 wood chopper processes 500 kg of fuelwood an hour, and its EWG 1 wood grab lifts a cubic metre of chips at a time.
